# Story, hook and script

## 1. Pick a premise people get in seconds
A short drama or ad has no time for set-up, so start from a situation the audience already knows how to feel about. Proven engines:

- **Cinderella**: someone ordinary gets an unexpected opportunity.
- **Love against the odds**: two people want each other, and something stands in the way.
- **Rivalry**: two people want the same thing, and only one can have it.
- **Hidden identity**: the person everyone underestimates is secretly powerful, rich or important.
- **Revenge or comeback**: someone humiliated returns transformed.
- **Wrong assumption**: someone is judged for one thing, and the truth is funnier or sweeter (this is the engine of most meme ads, for example "he isn't cheating, he just smelled the perfume").
- **Ticking clock**: something must happen before a deadline.
- **Fish out of water**: someone lands in a world with rules they don't know.

The premise has to rest on a **feeling everyone recognises**. For example: "I don't belong here, I just embarrassed myself, and now I'm in trouble", and then it becomes an opportunity instead. You don't need to know the industry to feel that. Name this feeling in one sentence before writing anything.

Premise test: can you pitch it in one sentence with a who, a want and a twist? If not, simplify.

## 2. Length and structure
- Short drama episode: 1 to 3 minutes, 4 to 8 scenes, each scene 10 to 30 seconds of generated footage.
- Social ad: 15 to 60 seconds, 15 to 35 shots of 1 to 3 seconds of screen time each.

### The four parts of every short drama
1. **Hook**: open on the peak, the loudest moment of the episode (a fall, an outburst, a slap, a reveal), shown without explanation. The viewer stays to find out how it got there.
2. **Context**: who these people are and what's at stake, told through action, not exposition. Keep emotional swings coming every 20 to 30 seconds: a starting point, then hope, then another hit, then a chance.
3. **Escalation**: an unexpected turn. A new character arrives, the conflict grows, something happens nobody saw coming. Raise the stakes for the protagonist.
4. **Cliffhanger**: end one beat before the answer everyone is waiting for: a decision, a reveal of who someone really is, a transformation about to be seen. Cut to black.

### Ad version (meme or story ads)
1. **Hook (0 to 3 s)**: a visual stopper plus a question the viewer wants answered.
2. **Misunderstanding or problem**: a relatable situation.
3. **Escalation**: two to four quick beats, getting more absurd or more emotional.
4. **Reveal**: the product is the real reason or the answer.
5. **Premium showcase**: switch tone completely (light, pace, music).
6. **CTA**: one clear line, the brand and the real link.

## 3. Hook formulas (first 1 to 3 seconds)
- **Peak first**: start on the most dramatic frame, then rewind ("2 hours earlier").
- **Wrong-impression freeze**: a moment that looks like one thing (cheating, a crime, a breakup), frozen with labels.
- **Physical comedy or accident**: a trip, a spill, a fall in slow motion.
- **Shocking line**: the first line of dialogue is the most quotable one ("Should I fire her?").
- **Impossible contrast**: an ordinary person in an extraordinary place, or the reverse.
- **Direct stare and question** to camera (for UGC).

Hook checks: is it readable on mute? Is the subject in the central safe area? Does it raise a question?

## 4. The biggest secret: let the audience catch every reaction
The tension comes from **two characters reading the same moment differently**. She thinks she's in trouble; he thinks he has found someone special. Always describe **both performances**, and always cut to the listener's reaction. Write reaction shots into the script as their own beats: side-eye, a pen stopping, a blink, a held smile.

## 5. Acting task (write one per character per scene)
Video models act much better with a clear inner life. For each character:
- **Direction**: the overall arc of this scene for them.
- **Motive**: why they feel this way (history, fear, desire).
- **Goal**: what they want in this scene.
- **Obstacle**: what stops them.
- **Tactic**: how it shows physically. Spell out the movement too ("he gets up from the red chair, leaves the table and crosses the floor toward her").

Example: "MOTIVE: everything around him is fake and he's tired of it. GOAL: find one unrehearsed thing. OBSTACLE: every face is the same. TACTIC: never raises his voice; irritation shows only as the voice getting quieter and flatter."

## 6. Dialogue rules
- Short, natural, interruptible. Subtext over explanation.
- One line per clip segment, each under 8 seconds (about 20 words).
- Lines start **while the movement is still happening**: speech rides on top of action, with no dead air between lines and no gesture-then-pause-then-talk.
- Running gags and repeated words build rhythm ("Next." getting quieter each time).
- Mark every line: speaker, on-screen or off-screen, delivery, timing window (for example 2.5 to 3.5 s).
- Only the quoted lines are spoken. Listeners keep their mouths closed.

### Voices and accents
Give every speaking character a voice description and carry it word for word into every scene. If an accent comes out wrong, describe **how it actually sounds**, not just the label. For example, a French accent in English: soft uvular "r" made at the back of the throat, dropped "h" ("'aven't you"), rounded vowels, stress on the last syllable, a melodic rise and fall, French words kept in French ("Non", "Bon"). For Malaysian speakers: natural Bahasa Melayu with casual English mixed in, Malaysian rhythm, not Indonesian and not formal. After generating, listen to all takes together to check that the voices match.

## 7. Script format the generator can use
For each scene:
```
SCENE 3: THE ESCALATION (0:45 to 1:10)  Location: @CastingRoom  Cast: @Raphael, @Lucy, @Rebecca
Purpose: escalation, he wants to know who she is
Beats:
 - 0.0-3.0s  CU @Lucy on the floor gathering pages, apologising nervously
 - 3.0-5.0s  CU @Raphael, boredom turning to curiosity; he rises from the red chair
 ...
Dialogue:
 RAPHAEL (off screen, soft French accent): "What's her name?"  (3.2-4.0s)
 REBECCA (flustered): "Uh, she's... she's new."  (4.2-5.4s)
Acting tasks: ...
Reactions to catch: Rebecca's pen stops; Lucy freezes mid-reach
Cut types: hard cuts; match cuts for repeated actions
Ends on: ...
```

## 8. Story idea bank (original starting points)
1. An intern accidentally sends the CEO's secret love letter to the whole company.
2. A delivery rider is mistaken for the heir at a billionaire's birthday.
3. A shy barista is the anonymous food critic every restaurant fears.
4. Two rival bakers are forced to share one kitchen for a televised final.
5. A bride discovers her wedding planner is her fiancé's ex.
6. A cleaner at a gallery fixes a "damaged" painting and it becomes the star of the show.
7. A girlfriend suspects cheating; he's only obsessed with a scent (product reveal).
8. A grandmother secretly runs a famous gaming channel.
9. A security guard catches the thief who turns out to be the owner's daughter.
10. A tutor is hired for a rich family's son and realises the father is her old classmate.
11. A street musician is filmed by a passing producer at the worst moment.
12. A man wakes up as his own assistant for a day.
13. Two neighbours fight over a parking spot and are later matched on a dating app.
14. A failed chef gets one night to save her late father's stall.
15. A model is fired for being "too ordinary" and returns as the face of a rival brand.

## 9. Script doctor checklist
- Hook in the first 3 seconds, readable on mute?
- An emotional swing at least every 20 to 30 seconds?
- Does every scene change something (status, knowledge or stakes)?
- Are both sides of each key reaction shown?
- Does every line earn its place, and is each line under 8 seconds?
- Is there one clear escalation surprise?
- Does it end one beat before the answer?
- For ads: is the product in the story early, is the reveal satisfying, and is the CTA readable?
